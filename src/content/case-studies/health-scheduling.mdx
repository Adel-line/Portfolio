## Context

Klinika sells scheduling software to private physiotherapy and dental clinics
across the Nordics. Clinics were churning, and exit interviews kept surfacing
the same number: patient no-shows sat at 19%, and clinics blamed the software's
booking and reminder experience. I shared the project with one other designer;
I owned the patient-facing side, she owned the clinic admin side, and we met in
the middle weekly.

## The problem, precisely

No-shows are a service problem wearing a UI costume, so we resisted jumping to
screens. Four weeks of research across six clinics — shadowing receptionists,
call logs, and 15 patient interviews — broke the 19% into causes:

- **~8 points: reminders people never saw.** SMS reminders went out 24 hours
  ahead from an unrecognizable sender number, and only in one language.
- **~6 points: appointments people couldn't move.** Rescheduling required
  calling during office hours. Patients who couldn't attend often just... didn't.
- **~5 points: appointments people forgot they'd made.** Bookings made by a
  receptionist on the patient's behalf never reached the patient's calendar.

<PullQuote attribution="Receptionist, Aarhus physiotherapy clinic">
  Monday mornings I spend an hour calling people who booked three weeks ago and
  forgot we exist.
</PullQuote>

That decomposition became the roadmap. Each cause got its own intervention,
shippable independently — important for a two-designer team at a company that
releases every two weeks.

## Process

We ran the three interventions as a sequence, cheapest first:

1. **Reminder rewrite (weeks 1–3).** Sender name instead of a number, patient's
   language, and a second reminder 3 hours before with a one-tap "I'm coming /
   I need to move it" response. No new screens — mostly copy and timing.
2. **Self-serve rescheduling (weeks 4–10).** The reminder's "move it" tap opens
   a slot picker scoped to the same practitioner. The design challenge was the
   clinic side: practitioners feared patients shuffling their calendar chaos.
   We added clinic-configurable rescheduling windows, tested with 5 clinic
   managers before a line of code.
3. **Calendar handoff (weeks 11–14).** Every booking — including ones made at
   the front desk — generates a calendar invite. Boring, unglamorous, five
   points of no-shows.

## Decisions and reasoning

**We said no to an app.** Leadership wanted a patient app; retention logic
seemed obvious. But our research said patients interact with a clinic 4–6 times
a year — nobody installs an app for that. Everything shipped over SMS and web,
no login required, magic-link auth for rescheduling. The decision doc for this
"no" is the artifact from this project I'm proudest of.

**Rescheduling limits were a negotiation, not a setting.** Early designs let
patients rebook freely; pilot clinics revolted within days. The shipped version
defaults to "up to 24h before, same practitioner, twice per appointment" —
found by iterating with clinic managers, not by A/B testing patients. B2B2C
means designing for the customer *and* the user, and being honest about when
they conflict.

## Outcome

Measured across 40 pilot clinics over 12 weeks, against a matched control group:

- No-shows: **19% → 9%**
- 71% of rescheduling now self-serve, saving front desks ~4 hours a week
- Clinic churn in the pilot group fell by a third the following quarter
- The reminder rewrite alone — three weeks of work — accounted for nearly half
  the improvement

The lesson I carry forward: decompose the metric before designing anything.
The 19% was never one problem, and treating it as one would have produced a
prettier booking flow and roughly the same no-show rate.
