## Context

Fraktflow makes software for mid-size freight forwarders. Dispatchers — the
people deciding which carrier moves which shipment — were working across five
surfaces: Fraktflow's own queue, two carrier portals, a rate spreadsheet, and
email. The company wanted a "control tower" dashboard. I was embedded in the
platform team for four months to figure out what that actually meant.

## The problem, precisely

I started with contextual inquiry: two full days sitting with dispatchers at
three customer sites, screen-recording (with consent) and timestamping their
work. The pattern was consistent and specific:

- A dispatcher handles **60–90 shipments a day**; roughly 80% are routine and
  take seconds.
- The other 20% are *exceptions* — a delayed pickup, a rate discrepancy, a
  customs flag — and each one cost **5–8 minutes of tool-switching** to
  assemble enough context to decide.
- Fullstory confirmed it at scale: the median "exception journey" touched 4
  surfaces and included a copy-paste into email 70% of the time.

The reframe that shaped everything: dispatchers didn't need a dashboard for
all shipments. They needed the **exception cases to arrive pre-assembled.**

<PullQuote attribution="Dispatcher, customer site visit, day 2">
  I don't need to see the 70 green ones. Show me the six that are on fire and
  everything I need to put each one out.
</PullQuote>

## Process

I wrote the core design principle on the first page of the spec: *every
exception must be resolvable from a single screen.* Then worked backwards:

1. **Inventory the decisions.** From the site-visit recordings I catalogued 14
   recurring exception types and, for each, the exact data needed to resolve it
   (carrier ETA, rate delta, customer SLA, contact). This spreadsheet, not a
   wireframe, was the project's first deliverable.
2. **Design the exception card.** One expandable card per exception, with the
   needed data pulled from the source systems and the two or three plausible
   actions as buttons — rebook, escalate, notify customer — instead of a
   copy-paste into email.
3. **Test with real mess.** We prototyped with each pilot customer's own
   shipment data, not clean demo data. Fake data hides the layout-breaking
   realities: 40-character consignee names, triple-digit rate discrepancies,
   exceptions arriving in bursts of 20.

## Decisions and reasoning

**Density was a feature, not a flaw.** My early layouts used the generous
spacing I'd default to in consumer work; dispatchers hated them. They scan
grids all day and wanted more rows per screen, not fewer. We settled on a
compact table with a strict "one glance" hierarchy — status color, then
exception type, then age — after three rounds of testing with actual
dispatchers rather than trusting my taste. Calm design isn't sparse design; in
an expert tool, information you have to scroll for is information hidden.

**We shipped the queue before the intelligence.** Engineering flagged that
live carrier-data integration was two quarters of risk. Instead of blocking on
it, v1 pre-assembled exceptions from data Fraktflow already had and left a
clearly-marked "open carrier portal" link for the rest. Imperfect and shippable
beat complete and hypothetical; the integration followed in v2 with the layout
unchanged.

## Outcome

Measured with the same Fullstory instrumentation, eight weeks post-launch
across four pilot customers:

- Median exception decision time: **6.1 minutes → 1.8 minutes**
- Tool switches per exception: **4 → 1.3**
- Copy-paste-into-email rate: **70% → 12%**
- Two pilot customers expanded their licenses the following quarter, citing
  dispatcher throughput in the renewal call

What I'd do differently: bring engineering to the site visits. The two
engineers who eventually watched the recordings redesigned their own API
priorities afterwards — that alignment in week 2 instead of week 9 would have
saved a sprint of building the wrong integration first.
